Q2.2.2 Notes

High-level description:
The program joins ten short example sentences into a training corpus, creates a
character vocabulary, and converts overlapping 40-character contexts into
next-character training examples. It trains an embedding + LSTM + dense-output
network with sparse categorical cross-entropy. During generation, it repeatedly
predicts one character, samples from the temperature-scaled probabilities, and
feeds that character back into the next context.

Demo behavior:
The demo prints the corpus length, vocabulary, training-example count, and final
loss. For temperatures 0.1, 0.5, 0.7, and 1.0, it displays the five most likely
first characters and generates a 180-character sample. Lower temperature makes
high-probability characters dominate; higher temperature makes less likely
characters more common and the output more varied. Since the corpus is tiny,
generated text often resembles the training phrases without always forming
valid sentences.

Q2.3 Code-generation prompt:

Create a complete, runnable Python program that recreates the character-level
text-generation demo in tiny_rnn.py. Use TensorFlow/Keras and NumPy, and do not
download data or use a pretrained model.

The training corpus must contain these ten lines, joined with newline characters:
I like cats.
I like dogs.
I like noodles.
I like tacos.
I like books.
I like robots.
I like music.
I like puzzles.
I like pizza.
I like coding.

Build a sorted character vocabulary and both character-to-index and
index-to-character mappings. Make overlapping next-character training examples
from a context window, then train a small recurrent neural network with an
embedding layer, an LSTM layer, and a dense output layer. Use integer labels and
a sparse categorical cross-entropy loss from logits.

After training, print the corpus length, vocabulary, number of training samples,
and final training loss. Implement autoregressive generation: at every step,
predict the next character from the most recent context window, divide logits by
temperature, apply softmax, sample one character, and append it to the context.
For temperature <= 0, choose the highest-logit character instead. Also print the
five most likely next characters and their probabilities for the initial prompt.
Run the demo at temperatures 0.1, 0.5, 0.7, and 1.0 and print a generated sample
for each. Use a fixed random seed so repeated runs can be compared.
